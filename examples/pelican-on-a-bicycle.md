# The Pelican and the Language Model

<!-- markleft:block id="b313996d" -->
On a bright morning beside the harbor, an LLM received an unusual prompt:

<!-- markleft:block id="b0aca3f9" -->
> Explain how to ride a bicycle—but write the answer for a pelican.

<!-- markleft:block id="b801f830" -->
The model considered balance, feathers, webbed feet, and the practical difficulty of fitting a long beak behind a handlebar. Then it produced twelve confident steps, three safety warnings, and a short poem about momentum.

## The first attempt

<!-- markleft:block id="bf2b9334" -->
Perry the pelican read every instruction carefully. He placed one webbed foot on a pedal, stretched his wings for balance, and pushed away from the pier. For several glorious seconds, bird and bicycle moved as one.

<!-- markleft:block id="be038357" -->
Then Perry discovered something the model had not mentioned: a pelican approaching a basket of fish will always turn faster than expected.

<!-- markleft:block id="bda5a964" -->
## A useful correction

<!-- markleft:block id="b9f83552" -->
Perry returned with a new prompt:

<!-- markleft:block id="b3c59c6f" -->
> Revise the instructions using what happened in the real world.

<!-- markleft:block id="b5cbd848" -->
This time, the model asked questions before answering. Together they added a wider basket, lower handlebars, and one essential rule: test advice in small steps before trusting it at full speed.

<!-- markleft:block id="ba81f680" -->
By sunset, Perry was circling the lighthouse path with a silver fish in his basket. The LLM had supplied the words, but the pelican had supplied the experience—and the best result came from letting each correct the other.

<!-- markleft:block id="bd7426f3" -->
## Moral

<!-- markleft:block id="bb54496f" -->
Language models can offer remarkably useful maps. Reality still decides where the road bends.
